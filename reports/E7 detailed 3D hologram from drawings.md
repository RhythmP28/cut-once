# Let E7's column grid generate the detail

The team can turn E7 from its current 17-block massing model into a recognisable architectural hologram before feature freeze. The way to do it is to *generate* detail from a handful of measured facts, not to reconstruct every line of the drawings. The original-resolution ArchDaily plates (3,438 px square, about 23.6 px/m) are clean monochrome CAD linework. They show the column grid, the grey service cores, stair treads, door swings, the "open to below" atrium voids and the roof's eight glazed sawtooth bays. In this session a 30-line script found **38 columns on Level 3 on a regular grid of about 8.7 m by 7 m**, and the measured **13.0 m column-to-column atrium** matches the civil engineer's published "12-metre-wide atrium". Ontario's open lidar adds what the drawings lack: roof terraces at about **31 m** and the penthouse at about **36.5 m** above grade. That shows the current model's top is roughly 3 m too low. With those inputs, the existing Python pipeline can emit about **70 named parts and roughly 30–80k triangles**, well inside Quest 3 limits: slabs with atrium voids, columns, per-floor curtain-wall frame and glass, stair cores, bridges, the penthouse and the sawtooth roof. Every alternative is slower or harder to defend in a pitch that claims "drawings → 3D → build sequence". Plan vectorizers trained on apartments shrink E7's doors to a few pixels. Gemini 3.1 Pro, the only frontier vision model measured on this, scores 0.47 wall F1 at tight tolerance. Phone capture can't see the roof and fails on glass. Google's 3D Tiles forbid tracing. The pipeline costs about 6–8 hours of agent-written code (Codex, which also feeds the OpenAI prize log), but only about 2 hours of Michael's time, a 25-minute photo walk by a teammate and about an hour of A2's Unity time. **After a hard gate at Sat 19:00 EDT, the existing massing model ships instead.** Every part should carry a source tag (drawings, lidar, photos or assumed), which turns the on-screen honesty caption from a disclaimer into a feature.

*How this was put together. Five research notes compiled on 2026-09-19 were combined with two earlier reports on blueprint reconstruction and reusable code. Three checks were run in this session on the files in the scratchpad: I viewed the Level 3, Level 8 and section plates at original resolution, ran a column detector on Level 3 and a bay-pitch profile on Level 8, and re-sampled the lidar height grid. Those measurements are marked "measured in this session". They are quick checks, not a survey, so the confirm clicks below still matter. All clock times are EDT. It is now Sat 02:10 (T+2:10), and feature freeze is Sun 00:00 (T+24).*

## The original plates encode E7's grid, voids and roof

**Swapping `large_jpg` for `original` in the ArchDaily image URLs gives 1.72× the resolution for free.** The Level 1–8 plans come back at 3,438–3,439 px square instead of 2,000 px, and the site plan jumps to 8,394 × 6,941 px ([LEVEL_01 original](https://images.adsttc.com/media/images/5fc0/5c12/63c0/17d6/2c00/10b6/original/LEVEL_01_PLAN_1-500-01.jpg)). At the pipeline's measured 13.75 px/m for `large_jpg`, the originals are about 23.6 px/m, so a 0.9 m door is about 21 px wide instead of 12. The one plate that does not improve is the E7 section: it is a 1,500 × 526 px rendered perspective at every size ([E7 section](https://images.adsttc.com/media/images/5fc0/5f64/63c0/17d6/2c00/10d3/original/UWaterloo_Engineering7_Section.jpg)). No vector PDF or DWG, elevation, wall section or detail of E7 was found anywhere public, and no existing 3D model of E7 exists on Sketchfab, 3D Warehouse or GitHub ([ArchDaily project page](https://www.archdaily.com/952235/university-of-waterloo-engineering-5-and-7-perkins-and-will)). The team's model would plausibly be the first public detailed one.

Looking at the plates changes one assumption in the vectorization research. **The E7 sheets are black-line CAD plates, not coloured presentation plans.** The only fills are dark-grey squares for columns and light grey for service cores and stairs ([LEVEL_03 original](https://images.adsttc.com/media/images/5fc0/5caa/63c0/17dd/6300/0d98/original/LEVEL_03_PLAN_1-500-01.jpg)). The "cluster the room colours" trick the notes proposed therefore doesn't apply. Clean monochrome linework is still the easiest possible input for classical detection. On the Level 3 original, a threshold on mid-grey pixels plus a connected-component filter for solid 10–45 px squares found **38 columns, each about 17 px (≈0.7 m) square** (measured in this session). Clustered into grid lines, they give **four bays of about 6.8, 7.2, 7.2 and 6.8 m across the E7 bar (≈28 m between column centres) and bays of about 8.7 m along it**. The atrium measures **13.0 m between column lines**, which agrees with MTE's description of "a 12-metre-wide atrium" ([MTE](https://mte85.com/case-studies/university-of-waterloo-engineering-7/)) once the column width is allowed for. That agreement independently confirms the pipeline's judgement call that E7 is the east bar plus the atrium, with the west wing being the existing Engineering 5.

The Level 8 plate is the richest single source for the roof ([LEVEL_08 original](https://images.adsttc.com/media/images/5fc0/5e41/63c0/17dd/6300/0da4/original/LEVEL_08_PLAN_1-500-01.jpg)). It shows a large mechanical penthouse with equipment pads, two stair cores and interior columns. It also shows roof terraces edged with pavers at the north, east and south, and, over the atrium, **eight glazed sawtooth bays alternating with opaque sloped panels**. A darkness profile along the atrium strip puts the bay pitch at **roughly 8.3–8.7 m**, the same as the structural bay (measured in this session, noisy). The section render also shows eight teeth, plus a red zig-zag feature stair, bridges at every level, and dark fritted panels on parts of the atrium's back wall ([E7 section](https://images.adsttc.com/media/images/5fc0/5f64/63c0/17d6/2c00/10d3/original/UWaterloo_Engineering7_Section.jpg)). Consultants' pages add the facts no plate states. The atrium is seven storeys with "a series of bridges at each level", and a two-storey robot lab sits beside it in structural glazing ([Smith+Andersen PDF](https://smithandandersen.com/sites/default/files/project_profiles/2022-05/Waterloo_Engineering7_07067.pdf)). The envelope is a unitized ENNWALL-70 curtain wall ([Ennova](https://ennovafacades.com/project/university-of-waterloo-engineering-7/)). Labs and offices wear a "fritted glass skin that emulates the pyramidal volumetrics" of the anechoic chamber, and public spaces use low-iron clear glass ([Perkins&Will](https://perkinswill.com/project/engineering-5-and-7/)). E6 connects by an enclosed bridge at the third storey ([MTE](https://mte85.com/case-studies/university-of-waterloo-engineering-7/)).

At 21 px, doors are clearly drawn with swing arcs, but they remain a small-object problem. They sit under COCO's 32 px "small" bucket, where a public floor-plan detector reports mAP 0.552 against 0.806 for medium objects ([RT-DETRv2 floorplan card](https://huggingface.co/Voix7/rtdetrv2-floorplan-v2)). **The honest ceiling for the next 22 hours: the drawings reliably give outlines, the column grid, voids, cores, stair positions, bridges and the roof layout. Individual doors and interior partitions are traceable, but only by hand, one floor at a time.**

## Lidar, photos and assumptions fill what the drawings omit

No published source gives floor-to-floor heights, the curtain-wall module, the sawtooth angle or the structural depth ([ArchDaily](https://www.archdaily.com/952235/university-of-waterloo-engineering-5-and-7-perkins-and-will); [Ennova](https://ennovafacades.com/project/university-of-waterloo-engineering-7/)). The pipeline currently reads floor lines off the perspective section. That gives a 4.83 m ground floor and about 4.2 m above, with Level 8's floor at 30.1 m and an assumed top at 33.6 m (`data/e7/stages/heights/heights.json`). **Ontario's lidar-derived DSM and DTM (0.5 m, 2025 acquisition, Open Government Licence – Ontario) turn that estimate into something checkable.** A 395 KB crop from the public ImageServer is already in the scratchpad. Inside the OSM outline of E7 it gives about 1,550 m² at a median of **31.0 m** and about 1,980 m² at a median of **36.5 m** above grade, with a maximum of 38.35 m ([LIO Elevation ImageServer](https://ws.geoservices.lrc.gov.on.ca/arcgis5/rest/services/Elevation/Ontario_DSM_LidarDerived/ImageServer); [OSM way 382735686](https://www.openstreetmap.org/way/382735686)).

Those areas match the Level 8 plan closely. The penthouse is about 24 × 69 m, roughly 1,600 m², plus some sawtooth ridges. The terraces plus the atrium roof account for the rest. **So the 31 m band is Level 8's roof terrace (the section's 30.1 m floor plus about 0.9 m of parapet and pavers), and 36.5 m is the penthouse top.** The section-derived floor lines agree with lidar to within about a metre, and the current model's 33.6 m top is about 3 m short of the penthouse. That is my reading of the two data sets, not something a document states. One number conflicts. The research note puts the sawtooth strip at 22–30.5 m. My re-sample of the height grid beside the E7 block found values oscillating between about **32 and 35 m** (ridges and valleys), and values in the note's 22–30 m range sit in a different part of the crop, so the note likely sampled the wrong strip. The fix is 15 minutes of work: register the traced atrium polygon to the crop and take the ridge height from the pixels inside it.

The team's own photos fill the facade gap, because **the team is inside the building this weekend**. Openly licensed photos of the finished E7 are almost nonexistent. Wikimedia has one CC BY-SA sign photo taken outside it ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?curid=86543830)). The high-resolution ArchDaily and Perkins&Will photos are all rights reserved ([ArchDaily](https://www.archdaily.com/952235/university-of-waterloo-engineering-5-and-7-perkins-and-will)). Counting mullions per 8.7 m bay on each facade type, noting where frit and spandrel bands fall, and taping one stair riser (then counting risers per flight to check floor-to-floor height) turns three assumed numbers into measured ones. Canada's Copyright Act expressly allows photographing and filming a building ([s. 32.2](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-42/section-32.2.html)). Anything left is a stated assumption: 0.3 m slabs, 70 × 150 mm mullions, a 0.9 m parapet, 175 mm risers.

The strongest case for a learned vectorizer is that it could, in principle, produce every interior wall on all eight floors with no clicking. Two facts sink it here. First, every public checkpoint expects small fixed inputs: 512 px letterboxed for the Yytsi UNet ([repo](https://github.com/Yytsi/floorplan-to-3d)) and 256 px for most Raster2Seq weights ([hub](https://github.com/Cornell-VAILab/Raster2Seq/blob/master/raster2seq_hub.py)). Fed a whole sheet, they shrink a door to 1.5–3 px. Run on tiles instead, they are out of domain on institutional plans. Zero-shot wall F1 for trained networks on an unseen benchmark was 0.62–0.69, against 0.92–0.97 after fine-tuning ([fpvec-lab](https://github.com/Cyprinus12138/fpvec-lab)). Second, interiors barely show in a curtain-walled hologram viewed from outside. **The detail that makes E7 read as E7 is the glass grid, the atrium and the sawtooth roof, and those come from a few numbers, not from pixels.**

The pattern that survives both the benchmarks and an honesty review comes from CAD2BIM: an agent writes a spec that records its evidence, a fixed exporter compiles it "without making any new semantic decisions", and the agent renders and compares ([CAD2BIM](https://github.com/alvin528/CAD2BIM)). Here the "spec" is `e7_overrides.yaml`. Its numbers come from detection plus human confirmation. The exporter is the Python generator, and the check is a geometry-only render laid next to the plate. Classical detection is the right tool because it is deterministic and the linework is clean. The same approach found the 38 columns in this session with nothing but numpy and Pillow, both already in `tools/e7/requirements.txt`.

## Seventy named parts keep the Quest and the 4D reveal happy

The generator extends the existing pipeline without changing its contracts. The contracts stay the same: one GLB node per `part_id`, coordinates baked into vertices, one Plan JSON, and one planned event per part. Five new stages carry the detail. `07_grid.py` detects column squares on each original plate, clusters them into grid lines in metres, and proposes each level's outline by snapping the clicked corners to the perimeter column lines plus a fixed curtain-wall offset. A human then only confirms. `08_lidar.py` reads the committed lidar crop, registers the atrium and penthouse polygons with two control points from OSM, and writes zone heights. `check.py` fails if a modelled roof differs from lidar by more than 1 m. `09_elements.py` holds the element builders. `10_plan.py` and the existing `06_events.py` emit parts, steps and events.

The builders need **no 3D booleans at all in Tier 1**, which keeps the existing Python 3.11 venv and its `trimesh<5` pin untouched. Atrium voids are shapely polygon holes before extrusion, and curtain walls are generated boxes and glass quads, not cut walls ([trimesh.creation](https://trimesh.org/trimesh.creation.html)). If Tier 2 ever cuts door openings, add `manifold3d` and make one `difference([wall] + cutters)` call per wall. Manifold cut 528 windows from a 100 m, 8-storey wall in 83 ms in the local test. Keep coordinates near the origin, because trimesh hands Manifold float32 vertices ([trimesh/boolean.py](https://github.com/mikedh/trimesh/blob/main/trimesh/boolean.py)). The existing frame already does that.

The model is built in three tiers, each gated so a failure never blocks the video.

**Split parts by floor and system rather than by element.** That gives part ids such as `part_e7_l03_slab`, `part_e7_l03_columns`, `part_e7_l03_core`, `part_e7_l03_cw_frame`, `part_e7_l03_cw_glass`, `part_e7_l03_bridges` and `part_e7_roof_sawtooth_glass`. This keeps the node count near 70, so glTFast's one-GameObject-per-node import stays well under draw-call budget. Meta's Quest 3 guidance is 200–300 draw calls for a "busy" app and 1.3–1.8M triangles ([Meta](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unity/unity-perf/)), and the research recommends the building use at most 150 calls and 300k triangles. Scaling from the prototype puts Tier 1 at roughly 30–80k triangles: about 160 mullions per floor, 38 columns per floor, and stair treads dominate. That is an estimate. `check.py` should assert both ceilings. A merged-mesh design with element ids in a UV channel would scale to thousands of elements, but it needs new shader work from A2 and gains nothing at 70 parts. Keep every `part_id` unique and export no animations; otherwise glTFast renames nodes with suffixes. Build the `part_id → Transform` map after `InstantiateMainSceneAsync`, not inside the `NodeCreated` event ([GameObjectInstantiator.cs](https://github.com/Unity-Technologies/com.unity.cloud.gltfast/blob/main/Packages/com.unity.cloud.gltfast/Runtime/Scripts/GameObjectInstantiator.cs#L176-L209)). If the GLB is ever compressed with gltfpack, pass `-kn -km` so named nodes survive ([gltfpack README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/zeux/meshoptimizer/master/gltf/README.md)).

**Record provenance on every part without touching the schema.** `Part.external_ids` is already an optional string-to-string map in `packages/schemas/src/factory.ts`, so each part can carry `{"source": "drawings|lidar|photos|assumed", "basis": "L03 plan, columns auto-detected, confirmed"}`. `layer` is a free string, so new layers such as `circulation`, `roof` and `context` need no schema change either. `check.py` should require a source tag on every part. Unity can then offer a **provenance tint**: two seconds of the video, or a toggle in the headset, where drawing-derived parts glow cyan, lidar-checked roofs amber, photo-derived facade detail green and assumed values grey. That shows judges exactly which layer came from where. The blueprint's hologram shader already takes per-renderer colours, so the tint is a palette entry, not new rendering code.

**Build order should look like construction, not like a stack of floors.** The blueprint's rules already say so. Columns on level N come before slab N+1. The envelope trails the structure by two floors. Glass follows its frame, and the roof comes last. A topological sort of `rests_on` gives the order. For a 30-second video beat, spacing events so each floor's systems overlap gives about 2 s per floor and a 16–20 s rise, close to the ~19 s the current 17 events produce. The existing `Reveal.cs` clips each part by world height from its renderer bounds, so the pivots-at-origin GLB rises correctly with no change (`apps/quest/Assets/CutOnce/AR/Rendering/Reveal.cs`).

On the headset, mullions are the one detail that doesn't survive tabletop scale. At 1:200 a 70 mm mullion is 0.35 mm, below one pixel at arm's length, so it shimmers whatever you do. Because frames and glass are separate nodes, the device build can hide `*_cw_frame` and draw the grid procedurally in the glass shader, while the Editor-rendered video keeps real geometry. For the glass, use additive blending, or alpha blending with **separate alpha blend factors**. Meta documents that joint factors square the alpha over passthrough ([Meta passthrough troubleshooting](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unity/unity-pt-troubleshooting/)). Skip Application SpaceWarp, because transparent custom shaders produce artifacts without motion vectors ([Meta AppSW](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unity/unity-asw/)). For the video, use a separate URP quality level with HDR and Bloom, record at 3840 × 2160, and downscale with ffmpeg for supersampled edges. HDRP's accumulation anti-aliasing is not worth a pipeline switch ([Recorder accumulation](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.recorder@5.0/manual/RecordingAccumulationMotionBlur.html)).

Two coordinate rules keep the upgrade from breaking what exists. The override pixels are in the 2,000 px `large_jpg` frame, with the shared origin at `(802, 445)`. New detection on the originals should therefore divide by each sheet's own ratio, derived from its scale bar (about 1.719), and store results in that same frame, not re-base everything. And the section is still a perspective render, so its floor lines stay tagged `estimated_from_section`, with lidar as the check, not the source.

Two judgement calls sit outside engineering. `data/e7/out/` is committed to a public GitHub repo. A geometry-only model re-modelled from the drawings is not the drawings. Still, Canada's exception for depicting buildings names paintings, drawings, photographs and films, not 3D models, and it excludes copies "in the nature of an architectural drawing or plan" ([Copyright Act s. 32.2](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-42/section-32.2.html)). A detailed E7 GLB in a public repo is therefore a step further than the massing model. The team may prefer to make the repo private until submission, or to git-ignore the detailed GLB. That is a risk flag, not legal advice. The caption should also say which building this is now: E7 was renamed the Pearl Sullivan Engineering Building in November 2025 ([Academica](https://academica.ca/top-ten/uwaterloo-engineering-receives-20m-renames-engineering-building/)). A caption that matches the tags above would read: *"Engineering 7 (now Pearl Sullivan Engineering). Geometry from Perkins&Will's published drawings via ArchDaily; heights from the drawings' section, checked against Ontario lidar; façade rhythm counted from our own photos; parts marked 'typical' use assumed standard sizes."*

## Conclusion

The research shifts where "detail" comes from. A building like E7 compresses into a few dozen numbers: a column grid, eight outlines, a handful of voids and cores, eight floor lines, two roof heights and a mullion count. A generator that is honest about where each number came from renders a more convincing hologram by tomorrow than any pixel-level reconstruction could. It also tells a better story, because "drawings → 3D → build sequence" is exactly what the pipeline does, with AI writing the code rather than inventing the building. The lidar check is the most underrated piece. It is the first independent measurement in the E7 pipeline, and on its first use it caught a 3 m error in the penthouse and a disagreement about the sawtooth height that a by-eye pass would have shipped.

The provenance tags are worth more than their cost. Judges score originality and technical ability, and most teams that show a reconstructed building will blur the line between measured, inferred and invented. A two-second shot where each part glows by its source makes the honesty caption visible. It also answers the obvious judge question ("how much of that is real?") before it is asked, and it reuses the same `part_id` spine that already ties the desk demo's search, copilot and history together.
